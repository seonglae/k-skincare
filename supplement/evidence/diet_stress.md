# Diet, Gut–Brain Axis, and Stress-Induced Hyperventilation / HPA Axis Dysregulation

**Clinical context:** Adult patient with stress-induced hyperventilation (occasional breath-holding/dyspnea), chronic fatigue, suspects diet as root cause. Question: what dietary changes and nutrient sufficiency are evidence-supported for HPA-axis stress regulation?

---

## Executive Summary

The strongest dietary lever is **shifting from Western to Mediterranean pattern** (Lassale 2019: ~33% lower depression risk; SMILES RCT: Cohen's d = -1.16), followed by **capping caffeine well below the 400 mg/day anxiety threshold and minimising refined sugar/alcohol** (Liu 2024; Knüppel 2017). Targeted nutrient sufficiency — **omega-3 EPA ≥2 g/day, magnesium repletion, B-complex (B6/B9/B12)** — adds benefit (Su 2018; Boyle 2017; Young 2019), with **psychobiotics (B. longum NCC3001; L. helveticus R0052 + B. longum R0175)** as gut–brain adjuncts. Critical caveat: **hyperventilation syndrome itself responds primarily to breathing retraining (Buteyko/Papworth), not diet** — diet addresses HPA substrate and metabolic triggers, but the breathing pattern is a learned behaviour requiring behavioural correction.

## 1. Mediterranean Diet vs Western Diet for Anxiety/Depression

### 1.1 Lassale C, Batty GD, Baghdadli A, Jacka F, Sánchez-Villegas A, Kivimäki M, Akbaraly T (2019)
- **Citation:** *Healthy dietary indices and risk of depressive outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.* Molecular Psychiatry 24(7):965–986. DOI: 10.1038/s41380-018-0237-8. PMID: 30254236.
- **Design + N:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 41 observational studies (20 longitudinal, 21 cross-sectional).
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Highest vs lowest adherence to Mediterranean diet / Healthy Eating Index / Alternative HEI / Dietary Inflammatory Index.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Mediterranean adherence (highest vs lowest, longitudinal): pooled **RR 0.67 (95% CI 0.55–0.82)** for incident depression. Lower Dietary Inflammatory Index: RR 0.76 (0.63–0.92).
- **Quality grade: A** (rigorously conducted SR/MA of prospective cohorts; observational ceiling).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Adopting a Mediterranean-style pattern reduces incident depression risk by ~33%; this is the highest-evidence dietary lever.

### 1.2 Jacka FN, O'Neil A, Opie R, Itsiopoulos C, Cotton S, Mohebbi M, et al. (2017) — SMILES Trial
- **Citation:** *A randomised controlled trial of dietary improvement for adults with major depression (the "SMILES" trial).* BMC Medicine 15:23. DOI: 10.1186/s12916-017-0791-y. PMID: 28137247.
- **Design + N:** 12-week, parallel-group, single-blind RCT; N = 67 (33 ModiMedDiet, 34 social-support control); 56 completers.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Modified Mediterranean diet (ModiMedDiet) with 7 dietitian sessions vs befriending/social-support visits matched for contact frequency.
- **Outcome + effect size:** MADRS depression score: between-group **Cohen's d = -1.16 (95% CI -1.73 to -0.59)**, p < 0.001. Remission rates: 32.3% diet vs 8.0% control. **NNT = 4.1**.
- **Quality grade: B** (first-of-kind RCT, small N, single-blind; large effect size).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Diet quality change alone produced a large effect on depressive symptoms within 12 weeks — actionable proof-of-concept for this patient profile.

## 2. Caffeine, Alcohol, Refined Sugar — Modifiable Anxiety Drivers

### 2.1 Liu C, Wang L, Zhang C, Hu Z, Tang J, Xue J, Lu W (2024)
- **Citation:** *Caffeine intake and anxiety: a meta-analysis.* Frontiers in Psychology 15:1270246. DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1270246. PMID: 38362247.
- **Design + N:** Meta-analysis of observational and experimental caffeine-anxiety studies.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Caffeine intake (low vs high dose) vs no/low caffeine.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Overall SMD = 0.94 (95% CI 0.28–1.60). Dose-stratified: low-dose SMD = 0.61 (0.42–0.79); **high-dose (>400 mg/day) SMD = 2.86 (2.50–3.22)**.
- **Quality grade: B** (heterogeneous studies, but clear dose-response signal).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Cap caffeine at ≤200 mg/day (≈1 brewed coffee); avoid the >400 mg threshold that triples anxiety risk.

### 2.2 Knüppel A, Shipley MJ, Llewellyn CH, Brunner EJ (2017)
- **Citation:** *Sugar intake from sweet food and beverages, common mental disorder and depression: prospective findings from the Whitehall II study.* Scientific Reports 7(1):6287. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-05649-7. PMID: 28751637.
- **Design + N:** Prospective cohort, 23,245 person-observations, 5-year follow-up of UK civil servants.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Highest vs lowest tertile of sugar intake from sweet foods/beverages.
- **Outcome + effect size:** **OR 1.23 (95% CI 1.02–1.48)** for incident common mental disorder in men in highest sugar tertile, adjusted for confounders.
- **Quality grade: A** (large prospective cohort, well-adjusted; observational).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Cut sugary drinks and confectionery — a male-specific 23% incidence increase is relevant for adult male patients.

### 2.3 Glycemic variability and anxiety/depression (supporting evidence)
- **Citation:** Penckofer S et al. *Does glycemic variability impact mood and quality of life?* Diabetes Technol Ther 14(4):303–10 (2012). DOI: 10.1089/dia.2011.0191. PMID: 22324383. Extension: Park HJ et al. *Risk of depression and anxiety disorders according to long-term glycemic variability.* J Affect Disord 343:163–170 (2023).
- **Design + N:** Cross-sectional + large Korean cohort (>200,000).
- **Outcome:** Higher glycemic variability → increased depression/anxiety incidence (HR ~1.1–1.3 highest vs lowest quartile).
- **Quality grade: B** (observational; counterregulatory adrenaline surges mimic panic).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Pair carbohydrates with protein/fat; prefer low-GI staples (oats, legumes, intact grains over white rice/refined carbs).

## 3. Gut Microbiome and the Gut–Brain Axis (Psychobiotics)

### 3.1 Pinto-Sanchez MI, Hall GB, Ghajar K, et al. (2017)
- **Citation:** *Probiotic Bifidobacterium longum NCC3001 Reduces Depression Scores and Alters Brain Activity: A Pilot Study in Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome.* Gastroenterology 153(2):448–459.e8. DOI: 10.1053/j.gastro.2017.05.003. PMID: 28483500.
- **Design + N:** Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial; N = 44 IBS patients with mild–moderate anxiety/depression.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** *B. longum* NCC3001 daily × 6 weeks vs placebo.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Depression reduction ≥2 points on HADS in **64% (14/22) probiotic vs 32% (7/22) placebo (p = 0.04)**. fMRI showed reduced amygdala/fronto-limbic response to negative stimuli. Anxiety: no significant effect.
- **Quality grade: B** (pilot RCT, small N; mechanistic fMRI corroboration).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Probiotic strain with biological-plausibility evidence; useful adjunct if any GI symptoms accompany the stress profile.

### 3.2 Messaoudi M, Violle N, Bisson JF, Desor D, Javelot H, Rougeot C (2011)
- **Citation:** *Beneficial psychological effects of a probiotic formulation (Lactobacillus helveticus R0052 and Bifidobacterium longum R0175) in healthy human volunteers.* Gut Microbes 2(4):256–61. DOI: 10.4161/gmic.2.4.16108. PMID: 21983070. Companion: *Br J Nutr* 2011;105(5):755–64 (PMID: 20974015).
- **Design + N:** Double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomised parallel-group trial; 55 healthy volunteers, 30 days.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** *L. helveticus* R0052 + *B. longum* R0175 (Probio-Stick, 3×10⁹ CFU/day) vs placebo.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Significant reductions in HSCL-90 global severity, somatisation, depression, anger-hostility; HADS global score lowered; **24-h urinary free cortisol reduced** (p < 0.05) in probiotic group.
- **Quality grade: B** (small RCT, healthy population, but objective HPA-axis marker improved).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Only psychobiotic RCT to show a downstream HPA-axis (cortisol) effect — directly relevant to HPA dysregulation concerns.

## 4. Omega-3 (EPA) for Anxiety

### 4.1 Su KP, Tseng PT, Lin PY, Okubo R, Chen TY, Chen YW, Matsuoka YJ (2018)
- **Citation:** *Association of Use of Omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids With Changes in Severity of Anxiety Symptoms: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.* JAMA Network Open 1(5):e182327. DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.2327. PMID: 30646157.
- **Design + N:** Meta-analysis of 19 RCTs/clinical trials, total N = 2,240 (1,203 omega-3, 1,037 control).
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Omega-3 PUFA (EPA-dominant or mixed) vs placebo.
- **Outcome + effect size:** **Hedges g = 0.374 (95% CI 0.081–0.666), p = 0.01** for anxiety reduction. Dose-dependent: benefit significant **only at ≥2,000 mg/day**; lower EPA proportion (<60% pure EPA) showed greater efficacy.
- **Quality grade: A** (meta-analysis of RCTs, large pooled N).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Target ≥2 g/day combined EPA+DHA (oily fish 3–4×/week or fish oil); a top-tier evidence supplement for anxiety.

## 5. Magnesium and B Vitamins for Stress, Sleep, Anxiety

### 5.1 Boyle NB, Lawton C, Dye L (2017)
- **Citation:** *The Effects of Magnesium Supplementation on Subjective Anxiety and Stress — A Systematic Review.* Nutrients 9(5):429. DOI: 10.3390/nu9050429. PMID: 28445426.
- **Design + N:** Systematic review of 18 studies (anxious samples, PMS, hypertensive, postpartum).
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Oral Mg (varying salts, 200–500 mg/day elemental Mg) vs placebo/control.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Positive effect in 4/8 anxious-sample studies, 4/7 PMS, 1/2 hypertensive; authors note "evidence is suggestive of beneficial effect" but overall quality is low.
- **Quality grade: C** (heterogeneous, suggestive only; biological plausibility strong — Mg modulates NMDA and HPA axis).
- **Clinical takeaway:** Sufficiency matters more than supplementation: prioritise food sources (pumpkin seeds, almonds, dark chocolate, spinach, oats, legumes). Consider 200–300 mg/day Mg glycinate at bedtime if sleep affected — low risk, plausible benefit.

### 5.2 Young LM, Pipingas A, White DJ, Gauci S, Scholey A (2019)
- **Citation:** *A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of B Vitamin Supplementation on Depressive Symptoms, Anxiety, and Stress: Effects on Healthy and "At-Risk" Individuals.* Nutrients 11(9):2232. DOI: 10.3390/nu11092232. PMID: 31527485.
- **Design + N:** SR/MA of 18 articles (16 RCTs, N = 2,015).
- **Intervention vs comparator:** B-complex (B6/B9/B12 ± others) vs placebo.
- **Outcome + effect size:** **Stress: SMD 0.23 (95% CI 0.02–0.45), p = 0.03 — significant**. Depression: SMD 0.15 (-0.01–0.32), p = 0.07 (trend). Anxiety: no effect (SMD 0.03).
- **Quality grade: A** (SR/MA of RCTs).
- **Clinical takeaway:** B-complex helps stress (especially in at-risk/deficient individuals) but is not a primary anxiety treatment. Dietary shifts (e.g. from East Asian to Western staples) can lower folate intake — verify B12 (vegetarianism, gastritis) and folate status before supplementing.

## 6. Hyperventilation Syndrome: Where Diet Stops, Behaviour Starts

### 6.1 Jones M, Harvey A, Marston L, O'Connell NE (2013)
- **Citation:** *Breathing exercises for dysfunctional breathing/hyperventilation syndrome in adults.* Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2013(5):CD009041. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD009041.pub2. PMID: 23728685.
- **Design + N:** Cochrane systematic review; only 1 small RCT (N = 41) met inclusion criteria.
- **Intervention vs comparator:** Breathing retraining (Papworth/Buteyko-style) vs control.
- **Outcome + effect size:** Single included trial showed improvement in Nijmegen Questionnaire scores and quality of life; pooled estimates not possible.
- **Quality grade: C** (evidence base is thin; widely used in physiotherapy practice nonetheless).
- **Clinical takeaway:** **Diet alone will not resolve hyperventilation syndrome.** The breathing pattern itself is a learned dysfunctional behaviour. Refer to a physiotherapist trained in the Papworth or Buteyko method; supplement with diaphragmatic breathing practice and CO₂-tolerance work. Dietary changes reduce the *triggers* (caffeine, hypoglycaemic swings, alcohol withdrawal-rebound, magnesium deficiency-driven hyperexcitability) but do not retrain the breath.

## Diet vs Behavioural Intervention

| Symptom / Driver | Diet helps | Behavioural intervention needed |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic fatigue, low mood | Mediterranean diet, omega-3, B12/folate sufficiency | Sleep hygiene, exercise |
| Background anxiety | Caffeine ≤200 mg, low-GI carbs, psychobiotics, magnesium | CBT if persistent |
| HPA-axis dysregulation | Probio-Stick formulation, sugar/alcohol reduction | Stress management, sleep |
| Hyperventilation / breath-holding | Indirect (remove triggers) | **Primary — Buteyko/Papworth retraining** |
| Acute panic / dyspnoea under stress | Indirect | **Primary — diaphragmatic breathing, CO₂ tolerance** |

## Actionable Priorities (evidence × actionability)

1. **Mediterranean pattern:** oily fish 3–4×/week, vegetables/legumes/nuts/olive oil, intact grains, minimal ultra-processed food (Grade A).
2. **Caffeine ≤200 mg/day**, no afternoon caffeine, no energy drinks (Grade B).
3. **Cut refined sugar and sugary drinks**; pair carbs with protein/fat (Grade A/B).
4. **Omega-3 EPA+DHA ≥2 g/day** from oily fish or supplement (Grade A).
5. **Verify nutrient status:** 25-OH-vitamin-D, B12, folate, ferritin, magnesium; supplement only if deficient (Grade A for stress).
6. **Trial psychobiotic** (Probio-Stick: *L. helveticus* R0052 + *B. longum* R0175) for 4–8 weeks (Grade B; only RCT showing HPA-axis cortisol reduction).
7. **Refer for breathing retraining** (NHS physio / Buteyko) — primary intervention for the hyperventilation pattern.
8. **Moderate alcohol** (≤14 UK units/week) — withdrawal-rebound surges mimic panic.
